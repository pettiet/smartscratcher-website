How to scratch for a
whole year
without ruining your life.

Scratch-offs can be fun. They can also absolutely not be fun, pretty fast, if you're not paying attention. Here's a simple way to keep it firmly in the fun column.

We'd be lying if we said we didn't want you to keep playing scratchers. We built an app about them. But we'd be worse than lying if we pretended there's no way to do this badly — because there absolutely is, and people do it every day.

Here's our honest pitch: scratchers are a legitimately fun form of cheap entertainment if you treat them like entertainment. They stop being fun the moment you start treating them like a financial plan. The line between those two modes is thinner than it feels. This is a simple guide to staying on the right side of it.

Rule 1 — set the number first.

1

Decide the budget before you walk in.

Pick a number you're genuinely comfortable with losing — because statistically, you probably will. A weekly number is often easier than a monthly one because it's more concrete. "$20 a week on scratchers" is a budget you can actually feel. "$80 a month" is vague enough that it quietly becomes $130.

Here's the important part: the budget is the ceiling, not the target. Just because you haven't spent the full $20 yet doesn't mean you're owed the rest. Unspent budget is just money you got to keep.

pick a number. stick to it. the number is allowed to be small.

Rule 2 — don't chase losses.

2

The "one more ticket" feeling is the warning sign.

You bought five losing tickets. You feel like you're close. You buy a sixth. You're not close. Scratch-offs don't have momentum. Every ticket is completely independent of every other ticket. The universe is not quietly setting up a win for you.

If you find yourself buying extra tickets specifically because the earlier ones lost, stop for that session. The feeling of "I need to get even" is the single most common way people accidentally spend way more than they meant to.

losses don't owe you a win. tickets aren't friends.

Rule 3 — pay the bills first.

3

Scratcher money comes out of fun money.

Not grocery money. Not gas money. Not rent money. Not the emergency fund. Scratchers come from the same bucket as "did I grab coffee out instead of making it at home." It's a small entertainment expense, which means it comes out of the part of your budget that exists for small entertainment expenses.

If you don't have a "small entertainment" line item in your budget, you don't have a scratcher budget either. That's not a scolding, it's just arithmetic.

if it's a choice between dinner and a ticket, it's dinner. every time.

Rule 4 — be honest about what's happening.

4

A ticket is a few minutes of entertainment. That's the product.

You are not buying a chance at wealth. You are buying a brief window of "maybe." That window closes in 30 seconds when you finish scratching. If you enjoyed those 30 seconds, you got your money's worth. If you didn't, that game isn't for you and that's okay.

The moment you start thinking "this is how I'm going to..." anything — pay off something, buy something big, retire — is the moment the product has stopped being what you're actually getting. Nothing on a scratch-off rack is a realistic path to any of those things. It's just not.

the ticket is the experience. the prize is a bonus, not a plan.

🚩 The signs it's stopped being fun

These matter. They're the things actual problem-gambling researchers look for.

  • You're hiding it from your partner or family. The spending, the winnings, how often you play. If you're hiding it, it's bigger than it should be.
  • You've borrowed money to buy tickets. Even a small amount. Even from yourself, in the form of "I'll put gas on the card and use cash for tickets."
  • You think about scratchers a lot between plays. Planning what you'll buy. Thinking about which games are "due." It shouldn't take up mental real estate.
  • You feel worse, not better, after playing. The whole point is a bit of fun. If it's consistently making you anxious or angry, the product is broken for you.
  • You've spent more than the budget, more than once. Once is a slip. Repeatedly is a pattern, and the pattern goes somewhere bad if you don't catch it.
